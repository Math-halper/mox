# Cooking, nutrition, and food safety

Health and wellbeing covers the physiology. This document is the practical side: what to keep in a kitchen, how to cook without a recipe, how to feed yourself cheaply and well, and how to avoid the mistakes that make people ill.

## Food safety: the part that actually matters

Bacteria multiply fastest between 4 °C and 60 °C (40–140 °F), the "danger zone". Keep hot food hot and cold food cold; do not leave cooked food at room temperature for more than two hours.

- Wash hands before handling food, and wash boards and knives after raw meat.
- Keep raw meat, poultry, and fish apart from ready-to-eat food, in the fridge and on the counter.
- Cook poultry to 74 °C (165 °F) internal; pork and ground meat to 71 °C (160 °F); whole cuts of beef can be served rarer.
- Cool leftovers quickly (shallow containers, not a big pot) and refrigerate within two hours.
- Reheat leftovers to steaming hot, once; do not reheat the same portion twice.
- Fridge at or below 4 °C (40 °F), freezer at −18 °C (0 °F). A fridge thermometer is cheap and worth it.
- When in doubt, throw it out. Food poisoning costs more than the meal.

## A kitchen that can feed you

Pantry basics: rice, pasta, oats, flour, dried lentils and beans, tinned tomatoes, tinned beans, oil, vinegar, salt, pepper, stock cubes, onions, garlic, tinned fish, peanut butter, soy sauce, spices you actually use (cumin, paprika, chilli, curry powder, oregano), and a sweetener.

Fridge: eggs, milk or a plant alternative, butter, cheese, yoghurt, a vegetable, a fruit, mustard, a jar of something pickled.

Freezer: frozen vegetables (as nutritious as fresh and never rot), frozen peas and spinach, bread, batch-cooked portions.

Tools that earn their space: one good knife and a board, a large pan, a small pan, a baking tray, a bowl, a colander, a spatula, a wooden spoon, measuring cups or a scale. Everything else is optional.

## Cooking methods, briefly

- Boil and simmer: pasta, rice, potatoes, soups.
- Sauté and fry: quick vegetables, proteins; medium-high heat, do not crowd the pan.
- Roast: vegetables and meats in a hot oven (200–220 °C); oil, salt, space between pieces.
- Steam: vegetables, dumplings; keeps texture and nutrients.
- Braise: tough cuts of meat or beans, liquid, low heat, long time.
- Grill or broil: fast, high heat for thin things.
- No-cook: salad, sandwiches, overnight oats, assembly bowls.

## The formula for a meal without a recipe

Any balanced plate combines:

1. A base: rice, pasta, bread, potato, noodles, tortilla, or a grain such as barley or quinoa.
2. A protein: eggs, beans or lentils, chicken, fish, tofu, or yoghurt.
3. Two vegetables, at least one of them cooked.
4. A sauce or fat: oil and acid (lemon, vinegar), a tomato sauce, a yoghurt sauce, or a spice mix.
5. Salt, acid, and something fresh or crunchy at the end.

Change the sauce and the spice and the same formula becomes curry, pasta, a stir-fry, a grain bowl, or a stew.

## Flavour rules that improve everything

- Salt in layers, not only at the end. Under-salted food tastes flat before it tastes salty.
- Acid lifts rich food: a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar at the end of cooking.
- Fat carries flavour: a little oil or butter finishes vegetables.
- Browning builds flavour: pat food dry, get the pan hot, and let it sit before stirring.
- Taste as you cook; season to the food you actually have.

## Cooking cheaply

Cost per meal falls fastest with: dried staples, tinned and frozen vegetables, eggs, lentils and beans, whole chickens or cheaper cuts cooked slowly, and buying what is in season. Batch cooking on one day converts a cheap shop into five lunches. Waste is the biggest hidden cost — plan meals, keep a list, and use the freezer before things turn.

## Nutrition in one page

You do not need a supplement stack, a cleanse, or a special diet unless a clinician says so. Reliable basics:

- Eat vegetables and fruit at most meals; variety matters more than any single "superfood".
- Get protein at each meal; it helps with satiety and muscle maintenance.
- Choose whole grains and legumes often; fibre feeds gut bacteria and steadies blood sugar.
- Prefer unsaturated fats (olive oil, nuts, seeds, oily fish) and limit processed meat and sugary drinks.
- Drink water. Hunger and thirst are easy to confuse.
- Alcohol and ultra-processed snacks are fine occasionally and harmful in quantity.
- Weight is governed by energy balance over months, not by single meals.

## Reading a label

Per 100 g makes products comparable. High sugar is over about 22.5 g per 100 g; low is under 5 g. High fat is over 17.5 g per 100 g; high salt is over 1.5 g per 100 g. Ignore front-of-pack claims such as "natural" and look at the ingredients list: ingredients are ordered by weight, so the first three tell you what the product mostly is.

## Coffee, tea, and hydration

Around 400 mg of caffeine a day is a common upper guideline for healthy adults, roughly four cups of coffee; less during pregnancy. Caffeine has a half-life of about five hours, so a 3 p.m. coffee still affects sleep at 8 p.m. Herbal infusions are caffeine-free. Tea contains tannins that reduce iron absorption, so leave a gap around iron-rich meals if you are anaemic.

## Food and mood, moods and food

Skipping breakfast is not a moral failing, but irregular eating often leads to grazing on snacks later. Eating slowly, at a table, without a screen is the most reliable way to notice fullness. Convenience is a real constraint: a frozen vegetable and a tin of beans beats an ideal that never happens.
