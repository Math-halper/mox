# Geography II: cities, climate, and maps

Geography I covers landforms, regions, and cultures. This document goes further into the human side: why cities grow the way they do, how climate systems work, how maps distort, and how to read population and resource data without being misled.

## Latitude, longitude, and coordinates

Latitude measures north–south position from the equator (0°) to the poles (90°). Longitude measures east–west from the prime meridian at Greenwich (0°) to 180°. Coordinates are given latitude first, then longitude, for example 30.27° N, 97.74° W for Austin, Texas.

Each degree divides into 60 minutes and each minute into 60 seconds. One degree of latitude is about 111 km; one degree of longitude shrinks toward the poles, from about 111 km at the equator to almost nothing at 90°.

## Time zones and the date line

Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, so 15° of longitude corresponds to one hour. Time zones zig-zag along political borders rather than pure meridians. The International Date Line near 180° separates calendar days: crossing it eastward, you repeat a day; westward, you skip one. Some places offset by half or three-quarter hours.

## Why cities exist and how they grow

Cities reduce the cost of interaction. Agglomeration economies mean firms and workers are more productive when close together: shared suppliers, larger labour pools, faster exchange of ideas. That explains clustering in finance, film, semiconductors, and tech.

Where cities form is shaped by site and situation: a defensible hill, a river crossing, a natural harbour, a rail junction, a coalfield. Many cities that boomed for a resource later had to reinvent themselves.

Internal structure follows patterns:

- Concentric zones: central business district, then older housing, then suburbs.
- Sector model: growth along transport corridors.
- Multiple nuclei: several centres, common in car-dependent cities.
- Edge cities: employment clusters outside the historic core.

Zoning separates uses; mixed-use districts put housing above shops. Euclidean (single-use) zoning, dominant in much of North America, tends to produce car dependence, while mixed-use, grid-based cities such as much of Europe support walking and transit.

Urban problems and tools: congestion (solved by pricing, transit, and land-use mixing), sprawl (low density raises infrastructure cost per person), housing cost (supply constraints, zoning, construction productivity), and heat islands (more pavement, less shade).

## Urbanisation trends

More than half of humanity lives in cities, and the share keeps rising, overwhelmingly in Asia and Africa. Megacities of over 10 million include Tokyo, Delhi, Shanghai, São Paulo, Mexico City, Cairo, and Lagos. Rapid urbanisation without infrastructure produces informal settlements; roughly a billion people live in them.

Counter-trends exist: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation to smaller towns, and "donut" effects where city centres hollow out, then revive through reinvestment and gentrification — which raises rents and can displace long-time residents.

## Climate systems

Climate is long-run weather; weather is the short-run state of the atmosphere.

Key drivers:

- Latitude: controls the angle of sunlight and therefore temperature.
- Altitude: about 6.5 °C cooler per 1,000 m.
- Distance from the sea: water heats and cools slowly, so coasts have milder, wetter climates than continental interiors.
- Ocean currents: the Gulf Stream warms western Europe; cold currents cool and dry coasts such as Namibia's.
- Prevailing winds and pressure belts: the trade winds, westerlies, and the polar front drive moisture patterns.
- Topography: windward slopes are wet (orographic rainfall), leeward slopes are dry (a rain shadow), as in the Himalayas and the Andes.

Climate zones, from the equator outward: tropical rainforest (hot, wet year-round), savanna (wet and dry seasons), desert (dry, large daily temperature range), Mediterranean (mild wet winters, hot dry summers), humid subtropical, temperate maritime, continental, polar and tundra. Köppen classification is the standard shorthand.

Monsoons are seasonal wind reversals that bring heavy summer rain to South and Southeast Asia. El Niño and La Niña are multi-year shifts in Pacific Ocean temperatures that move rainfall and affect harvests worldwide.

## Biomes and soils

Biomes are large communities shaped by climate: tropical rainforest, savanna, desert, temperate grassland, temperate forest, taiga (boreal forest), and tundra. Soil follows climate and time: thick organic-rich soils in grasslands, thin acidic soils in rainforests, frozen permafrost in the tundra.

## Maps and projections

Every flat map distorts something, because a sphere cannot be flattened without stretching. Choose the projection for the job:

- Mercator: preserves angles and shapes locally, ideal for navigation, but inflates high latitudes enormously — Greenland looks the size of Africa on a Mercator map.
- Equal-area (Mollweide, Eckert): preserves relative areas, distorts shape.
- Robinson and Winkel Tripel: compromise projections used for world maps.
- Azimuthal: centred on a pole, useful for polar routes.

Map essentials: scale (ratio of map to ground), legend, orientation, and a title. A large-scale map shows a small area in detail; a small-scale map shows a large area coarsely.

Contour lines join points of equal elevation; closely spaced contours mean steep ground. In digital mapping, GIS layers combine data such as roads, census tracts, and elevation, and remote sensing from satellites supplies the imagery.

## Population and development data

- Population growth rate = births − deaths + net migration, usually per 1,000 people per year.
- Total fertility rate is the average number of children per woman; about 2.1 replaces a population without migration.
- Doubling time is roughly 70 divided by the annual percentage growth rate.
- Life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, and GDP per capita are the usual development indicators; the Human Development Index combines health, education, and income.
- The demographic transition runs from high birth and death rates, through falling death rates and rapid growth, to low birth and death rates and ageing populations.

Read statistics critically: averages hide inequality, GDP ignores unpaid work and environmental cost, and a national figure can describe almost no individual in it.

## Resources and trade

Resource distribution drives geopolitics: oil and gas, rare earth elements, fresh water, arable land, and lithium and cobalt for batteries. Comparative advantage explains why countries specialise and trade, while tariffs, quotas, and sanctions shape what actually crosses borders. Container shipping made global supply chains cheap, and disruption at a single chokepoint — Suez, Panama, a strait, a port — can ripple worldwide.
