# Modern history: 1900 to today

A working timeline of the last century and a quarter, with the causes that matter more than the dates. Dates are anchors, not the point.

## Before the First World War

1900–1914 is the height of European empires: Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire hold most of Africa and much of Asia between them. Industrial power, nationalism, alliance blocs (Triple Alliance against Triple Entente), naval rivalry, and a scramble for colonies create a system where a local crisis can go global.

## The First World War, 1914–1918

The trigger is the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo in June 1914; the cause is the alliance system, mobilisation timetables, and imperial competition. Trench warfare on the Western Front, machine guns, artillery, and poison gas produce casualties in the tens of millions.

1917 brings two hinges: the Russian Revolution overthrows the tsar and eventually brings the Bolsheviks to power, and the United States enters the war. The armistice on 11 November 1918 ends the fighting. The Treaty of Versailles imposes reparations and territorial losses on Germany and creates the League of Nations; many historians see its terms as fuel for the next war.

## The interwar years

The 1920s boom in the United States ends with the 1929 stock market crash and the Great Depression, which spreads worldwide. Mass unemployment discredits liberal economics and helps authoritarian movements: Mussolini in Italy from 1922, Stalin consolidating power in the Soviet Union, Hitler becoming chancellor of Germany in 1933. The Spanish Civil War, 1936–1939, is a preview.

## The Second World War, 1939–1945

Germany invades Poland in September 1939; Britain and France declare war. Blitzkrieg overruns much of Europe by 1941. The war becomes global with the German invasion of the Soviet Union and the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941. Turning points: Stalingrad, El Alamein, Midway, and Kursk. The Holocaust murders six million Jews and millions of others. D-Day opens the Western Front in 1944. Germany surrenders in May 1945; atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August end the Pacific war.

## The Cold War

The United States and the Soviet Union become rival superpowers, with nuclear weapons, competing ideologies, and proxy wars. Institutions are created: the United Nations (1945), the Bretton Woods system, NATO (1949), and the Warsaw Pact (1955).

Key episodes: the Berlin Blockade and airlift, the Chinese Revolution of 1949, the Korean War, the Suez Crisis, the Berlin Wall (1961), the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962), the Vietnam War, Soviet and American interventions across Africa and Latin America, the Sino-Soviet split, and détente followed by renewed tension.

Decolonisation reshapes the map: India and Pakistan independent in 1947, most of Africa in the late 1950s and 1960s, and later waves in the 1970s. New states face borders drawn for imperial convenience.

The Cold War ends with Soviet economic strain, the Afghan war, and reform under Gorbachev (glasnost and perestroika); the Berlin Wall falls in 1989, and the Soviet Union dissolves in 1991.

## Globalisation

From the 1970s, container shipping, cheaper communication, and trade liberalisation spread production across borders. China's reforms from 1978 turn it into the world's manufacturing centre and, by the 2010s, its second-largest economy. Supply chains deepen, and with them interdependence.

## Technology as history

The integrated circuit, the personal computer, the internet (ARPANET 1969, the World Wide Web 1991), mobile phones, and machine learning each reshape work and politics. The printing press analogy holds: communication technology changes who can organise.

## After 1991

- European integration: the euro from 1999, EU enlargement in 2004 and after.
- 11 September 2001 attacks lead to wars in Afghanistan and Iraq and to the "war on terror".
- The 2008 financial crisis begins with mortgage debt and spreads through banking; the recovery is slow and unequal.
- The Arab Spring from 2010 topples several governments and turns into civil war in Syria.
- The 2015 Paris Agreement sets climate targets; emissions keep rising in most years.
- The COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 kills millions and accelerates remote work and vaccine technology.
- Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 reshapes European security and energy policy.

## How to read the century

Beware tidy narratives. Progress in health, literacy, and life expectancy is real and enormous — global life expectancy roughly doubled from 1900 — and so is the century's capacity for organised violence. Correlation between an event and an outcome is not causation: the 1919 Versailles settlement, the 1929 crash, and the 2008 crisis each had many causes, and historians still argue about weights. When you meet a claim about the past, ask: who recorded it, who was left out, and what would count as evidence against it.
