# Philosophy and critical thinking

Philosophy asks foundational questions and demands explicit reasoning. Critical thinking is the everyday version: forming beliefs in proportion to evidence.

## Branches

- Metaphysics: what exists? Mind, matter, causation, time, freedom.
- Epistemology: what is knowledge? Belief, truth, justification, testimony.
- Ethics: what should we do? Right action, character, value.
- Logic: what follows from what?
- Aesthetics: what makes something art or beautiful?
- Political philosophy: justice, authority, liberty, equality.

## Logic

An argument is a set of premises offered in support of a conclusion.

- Deductive validity: the conclusion must follow if premises are true. Soundness = valid + true premises.
- Inductive strength: premises make the conclusion probable.
- Common forms: modus ponens (if P then Q; P; therefore Q), modus tollens (if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P), disjunctive syllogism (P or Q; not P; therefore Q), and syllogism (all A are B; all B are C; therefore all A are C).

Fallacies frequently worth naming: affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, circular reasoning, equivocation, false dilemma, straw man, ad hominem, appeal to authority, appeal to popularity, slippery slope, hasty generalization, post hoc, and burden-of-proof shifting.

## Ethics: three families

- Consequentialism: rightness depends on outcomes (utilitarianism maximizes total welfare).
- Deontology: some duties and rules bind regardless of outcome (Kant's categorical imperative: act only on principles you could will as universal law; treat people as ends, not merely means).
- Virtue ethics: focus on character and habits; what would a person of practical wisdom do?

Applied questions include consent, fairness, honesty, harm, autonomy, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. Thought experiments (trolley problem, veil of ignorance) test intuitions by isolating a variable.

## Epistemology

Knowledge is traditionally justified true belief, though Gettier cases complicate that. Rationalism trusts reason; empiricism trusts observation. Skepticism challenges certainty. Occam's razor prefers simpler explanations with equal predictive power. Bayesian reasoning updates credences with evidence.

## Arguments about minds and reality

Dualism (mind separate from body), physicalism (mind is physical), functionalism (mental states are defined by role). Free will debates contrast determinism, compatibilism, and libertarian freedom. Realism holds that the world exists independent of observers; idealism denies it; pragmatism judges ideas by practical consequence.

## Critical thinking in practice

1. Identify the claim precisely.
2. Separate claim from evidence.
3. Ask what evidence would count against it.
4. Consider the strongest opposing case, not the weakest.
5. Check sources for expertise, bias, and incentives.
6. Distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions.
7. Watch hidden assumptions and loaded language.
8. State your confidence, not just your conclusion.

## Reasoning traps

Confirmation bias (seeking agreeing evidence), anchoring (first number dominates), availability (vivid examples feel common), survivorship bias (only successes are visible), groupthink, and motivated reasoning. Steelman opposing views; argue against the best version.

## Writing philosophy

State the thesis, define terms, present the argument in numbered or clearly signposted steps, address the strongest objection, and reply. Clarity is not decoration; it is how a reader checks whether the reasoning works.
