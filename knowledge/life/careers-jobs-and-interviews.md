# Careers, jobs, and interviews

This is practical guidance for finding work, applying well, and doing the interview. It is not career advice for one industry; it is how hiring actually works in most organisations that use applications and interviews.

## Know what you are selling

Employers buy evidence that you can do the job and will be easy to work with. Every application should answer two questions: what can you do, and what proof exists?

Build an inventory before writing anything:

- Skills you can demonstrate today, with an example for each.
- Outcomes you produced: numbers where possible (students taught, tickets closed per week, revenue, time saved, error rate cut).
- Tools and methods you know well enough to be asked about.
- What you want next: role, level, and the working conditions that matter.

## Finding roles

Most openings are filled through referral and internal moves before they are perfect matches on paper. Practical moves:

- Tell a specific set of people what you are looking for, in one sentence.
- Apply where you are 70% qualified; job descriptions are wish lists.
- Prefer organisations where you can name the problem you would be solving.
- Track applications in one place: company, role, date, contact, status, next action.

Signals to check before applying: is the role funded and specific, does the team exist, does the company pay on time and treat people decently, and does the job description describe outcomes or just a list of technologies?

## The CV or résumé

One or two pages, reverse chronological, no photographs, no objective statement.

- Header: name, one contact method, city or "remote", link to a portfolio or profile.
- Summary: two lines, only if it says something specific.
- Experience: for each role, title, company, dates, then three to five bullets. Each bullet is an action plus a result. "Rebuilt the onboarding flow, cutting drop-off from 41% to 18% in six weeks" beats "responsible for onboarding".
- Skills: grouped and honest; do not list what you would need to look up.
- Education and certification: brief unless you are early-career.

Write for a reader who spends 20 seconds: the top third of page one does the work. Match the vocabulary of the posting so both people and keyword filters recognise it. Export as PDF with a filename like `firstname-lastname-role.pdf`.

## Cover letters

Only when asked, or when the role clearly warrants it. Three short paragraphs: why this organisation and this problem, why you, and one concrete thing you did that maps to their need. Under 250 words, no restating the CV.

## Applying

- Apply early; many pipelines fill within weeks.
- Follow the stated process exactly — portals, file names, question limits.
- If you have a contact, ask a specific question rather than for "a referral".
- If asked for salary expectations, give a researched range and say it is negotiable.
- Record what you sent; assume nothing about follow-up timing.

## Preparing for interviews

Research: what the organisation makes or does, who its customers are, how it earns money, recent news, and the role's likely first six months. Prepare five stories that cover conflict, failure, leadership, ambiguity, and a technical or craft win.

The STAR structure keeps answers tight: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Spend most of the words on Action and Result, and finish with what you learned.

For technical roles, practise out loud on a whiteboard or shared editor, and narrate your thinking. For any role, prepare two or three questions about the work: how success is measured, who you would report to, what has gone wrong recently and why.

## Common interview formats

- Screening call: motivation, availability, salary range. Fifteen to thirty minutes.
- Behavioural interview: stories about past work.
- Skills test or take-home: time-boxed, documented, and worth doing only if the scope is clear.
- Panel or loop: several interviews in one day, each with a different focus.
- Case interview: a business problem worked through aloud.
- Presentation: ten minutes on a topic or a past project.

## Answering well

Answer the question that was asked, then stop. Rambling reads as unclear thinking. When you do not know, say what you do know and how you would find out. When you failed, describe the failure honestly and the change you made afterwards. Never criticise a former employer by name. Ask for clarification when a question is ambiguous; that is what good colleagues do.

## Your own questions

Ask about the work, not only the perks:

- What does success look like in the first three months?
- How are decisions made when people disagree?
- What is the biggest risk to this team right now?
- How do people get feedback?

## Offers and negotiation

Negotiate the whole package: base pay, bonus, equity, holiday, flexibility, training, and start date. It is normal and expected to ask for time to consider, and to negotiate once with a clear, reasoned number. Get the details in writing before resigning anything. A polite counter that cites market evidence is rarely punished; silence and hope are.

## Rejection and recovery

Rejection is usually about fit, timing, or internal candidates, not your worth. Ask for one piece of feedback in a short, gracious message. Keep a pipeline of several roles so no single outcome decides your month. After each interview, write down what they asked and where you stumbled; the next one gets easier.

## Early in a new job

The first ninety days set your reputation: ask questions, deliver something small and visible, meet people outside your team, and write down how things work while you are still new enough to notice. Ask your manager early what "doing well" looks like in their words.

## Freelancing and contract work

Price by project or day rate, not by hope. Use a written scope with deliverables, revision limits, payment schedule, and what happens on cancellation. Take a deposit for new clients. Keep records of hours and expenses from day one, and set aside tax as you earn it, because nobody will do it for you.

## Red flags in an employer

Unpaid extended "tests", vague role descriptions, pressure to start before an offer is in writing, high turnover visible in six months of job postings, and interviews where nobody can describe what the team does. Any one can be bad luck; three together is a pattern.
