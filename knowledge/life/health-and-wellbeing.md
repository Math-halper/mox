# Health and wellbeing

Health is more than the absence of illness: it includes physical function, mental state, and social connection. This is general information, not medical advice.

## Sleep

Adults generally need 7–9 hours. Sleep supports memory consolidation, immune function, mood, and metabolism. Habits that help: consistent wake time, morning daylight, caffeine cut off 8–10 hours before bed, cool dark quiet room, and a wind-down routine without screens. Persistent insomnia, loud snoring with pauses, or extreme daytime sleepiness deserves medical attention.

## Nutrition

- Energy balance: weight changes when intake and expenditure differ over time.
- Protein: roughly 0.8 g per kg of body weight daily for most adults; more for athletes and older adults.
- Fiber: 25–35 g daily from vegetables, fruit, beans, and whole grains supports digestion and heart health.
- Fats: prefer unsaturated (olive oil, nuts, fish); limit trans fats.
- Sodium: most people benefit from under about 2,300 mg per day.
- Hydration: water needs vary; pale-yellow urine is a reasonable sign.
- Dietary patterns with strong evidence: plenty of vegetables and fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, and limited processed meat and added sugar — Mediterranean-style eating is a well-studied example.
- Supplements are not a shortcut; discuss them with a clinician, and avoid megadoses.

## Confidence and self-esteem

Self-esteem is the story you tell about your own worth; self-compassion is how you treat yourself when that story sours. Evidence favours self-compassion over forced positivity: people who talk to themselves as they would to a friend recover from setbacks faster, procrastinate less, and try again sooner.

Practical levers: notice and name the inner critic instead of obeying it; separate a behaviour from an identity ("I missed the deadline" is not "I am unreliable"); keep a short record of things you finished, because memory keeps failures longer than wins; compare yourself with your past self rather than with someone's edited highlights; and act before you feel ready, since confidence usually follows evidence of doing the thing, not the reverse.

Social media amplifies upward comparison, which reliably lowers mood. Time-boxing feeds, muting accounts that make you feel small, and deliberately following people who are honest about failure are cheap fixes with real effects.

Self-esteem that depends on constant achievement is fragile. Sleep, exercise, daylight, and a couple of relationships where you can be unimpressive are less glamorous and more reliable foundations. Low mood, persistent self-criticism, or hopelessness that lasts weeks deserves professional help, not more willpower.

## Movement

Guidelines for adults: about 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week (or 75 vigorous), plus muscle-strengthening twice weekly, plus balance work for older adults.

Benefits: lower risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, several cancers, depression, and falls; better sleep and cognition. Walking counts. Consistency beats intensity; start below your limit and build gradually. Warm up, progress slowly, and rest when something hurts sharply.

## Mental health

Emotions are information, not failures. Evidence-based supports: psychotherapy (CBT and others), medication when indicated, social connection, sleep, exercise, daylight, and structure. Breathing and grounding techniques help acute anxiety; journaling and behavioral activation help low mood.

Warning signs to take seriously: persistent low mood or anhedonia for weeks, severe anxiety, hopelessness, thoughts of self-harm, major changes in sleep or appetite. Seek professional help. In the US, 988 is the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; emergency services handle immediate danger.

## Stress and recovery

Short-term stress is normal; chronic stress damages sleep, immunity, and cardiovascular health. Recovery comes from sleep, social contact, movement, time outdoors, and deliberate breaks. Box breathing (inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4) and progressive muscle relaxation are quick tools.

## Preventive care

Vaccinations, routine blood pressure and cholesterol checks, cancer screening at recommended ages, dental care, vision checks, and skin checks. Know your family history. Do not skip emergency care; early treatment is usually better.

## Substances

- Tobacco: no safe dose. Cessation tools include counseling, nicotine replacement, and prescription medication.
- Alcohol: risk rises with amount; less is better. Never mix with driving, pregnancy, or certain medications.
- Cannabis and other drugs: short- and long-term effects on memory, motivation, and mental health are real, especially for adolescents.
- Caffeine: moderate use is fine for most adults; it worsens anxiety and sleep if overused.

## Common conditions at a glance

Type 2 diabetes (insulin resistance; managed with activity, diet, medication), hypertension (often silent; lifestyle plus medication), asthma (airway inflammation; reliever and controller inhalers), depression and anxiety disorders, and infections (antibiotics work only for bacteria). Allergies involve immune overreaction; anaphylaxis is an emergency requiring epinephrine.

## First aid and safety

For bleeding, apply firm direct pressure. For burns, cool running water for 20 minutes, no ice. For suspected stroke, note the time symptoms began and call emergency services. CPR: hard, fast compressions at about 100–120 per minute. Wear helmets and seatbelts; store medications and firearms safely.

## Everyday wellbeing

Strong relationships are among the strongest predictors of wellbeing. Volunteer, keep contact with friends, spend time outside, limit doom-scrolling, and protect sleep. Small consistent habits outperform dramatic overhauls.
