# Everyday law, contracts, and civics

General information about how legal systems work, the documents ordinary people sign, and how government is organised. It is not legal advice: law varies by country and state, deadlines are unforgiving, and a local lawyer or legal-aid service is the right tool for a real dispute.

## How legal systems are structured

Most countries with a written or partly written framework separate powers: a legislature makes law, an executive administers it, and courts interpret it. Constitutional rules sit above ordinary statutes, and courts can strike down laws that conflict with them.

Two big families:

- Common law (England and its former colonies, the United States except Louisiana, Canada except Quebec, Australia, India in part): past decisions by courts bind future ones (precedent), alongside statutes.
- Civil law (most of continental Europe, Latin America, East Asia): comprehensive codes, with precedent persuasive rather than binding.

In the United States, federal law and state law are separate systems; states generally control contracts, property, family, and criminal law, while the federal government covers immigration, patents, bankruptcy, and interstate commerce. In the European Union, member-state law coexists with directly applicable EU law.

## Courts and what they do

- Trial courts hear facts and apply law; appellate courts review the law, not the facts.
- Civil cases are disputes between parties, decided on the balance of probabilities, with money or orders as the remedy.
- Criminal cases are brought by the state, decided beyond reasonable doubt, with punishment as the outcome.
- Small-claims courts handle modest money disputes quickly and without lawyers.
- Administrative tribunals review government decisions, benefits, permits, and immigration matters.

Limitation periods set deadlines for filing: often two to six years for contracts and injury claims. Missing one usually destroys the claim.

## Contracts

A contract needs offer, acceptance, consideration (something of value exchanged, in common-law systems), and an intention to be legally bound. Written contracts are not always required, but they matter for anything expensive.

What to read for, in any document you sign:

- Parties: exactly who is bound, including personal guarantees.
- Scope: precisely what is being delivered, with dates.
- Price, payment terms, and late fees.
- Term and termination: notice period, and what happens to money and data afterwards.
- Liability: caps, exclusions, and indemnities that shift risk to you.
- Dispute resolution: courts, arbitration, or mediation, and where.
- Renewal: automatic rollovers are usual in subscriptions and service contracts.
- Anything "incorporated by reference": the linked policy is part of the deal.

Never sign a document you have not read. Initial every page of an important one. Keep your own signed copy.

## Consumer rights

Consumer protection usually gives rights that cannot be signed away: goods must match their description, be fit for purpose, and last a reasonable time. In the EU and UK, distance purchases have a cooling-off period (around 14 days) except for personalised or perishable items. Credit cards often include chargeback protections; in the US, the Fair Credit Billing Act allows disputes for goods not delivered. Warranties are extra promises on top of those rights, not replacements.

## Renting a home

A tenancy agreement covers rent, deposit, notice, repairs, and who pays utilities. Before signing: photograph every room and record meter readings; confirm the deposit is held in a protection scheme where required; check how rent can be increased and how notice is served; confirm what happens if you want to leave early. Keep all communication in writing — messages are evidence.

Tenants usually have a right to a habitable home: heating, plumbing, safety, and structural repair. Report faults in writing and keep a copy.

## Privacy and data

Many jurisdictions give people rights over their personal data: to know what is held, get a copy, correct it, delete it, and object to certain uses. In Europe the GDPR requires a lawful basis, limits retention, and mandates breach notification; in the US, rules are sector-specific, with stronger protections for health, credit, and children's data, and state laws such as California's adding consumer rights.

Practical steps: use a password manager, turn on two-factor authentication, check app permissions, and read the retention sentence of any privacy policy before uploading sensitive documents.

## Work and money basics

Employment law sets minimums on pay, hours, safety, leave, notice, and protection from discrimination. Most countries distinguish employees (rights and protections) from independent contractors (autonomy, no protections). Written statements of terms are usual within weeks of starting.

Keep records: payslips, contracts, hours, expenses. If a dispute starts, resolve it in writing first, then use the internal process, then an employment tribunal or ombudsman.

Taxes are usually self-assessed in part: report income, claim allowable expenses, meet deadlines. Penalties for lateness dwarf the tax in small cases.

## Government and how to influence it

Democracies differ in structure — parliamentary systems fuse executive and legislature, presidential systems separate them — but the mechanics of influence are similar:

- Local government handles schools, roads, planning, waste, and often housing: it is the most accessible level.
- Public consultations accept written comments with deadlines; a clear, factual submission carries more weight than volume.
- Freedom-of-information requests can reveal documents held by public bodies.
- Elections, primaries, and party membership decide who is on the ballot.
- Courts and ombudsmen review decisions that ignore procedure or law.
- Nonprofits, unions, and professional bodies aggregate individual voices.

## If you have a legal problem

1. Write the timeline: dates, people, what was agreed, what went wrong, what you want.
2. Gather evidence: contracts, receipts, messages, photographs, recordings where lawful.
3. Try a documented, polite resolution first, with a deadline.
4. Use free help: legal aid, law-school clinics, tenants' unions, consumer bodies, ombudsmen.
5. Check deadlines early — they are the most common reason a good case fails.
6. Consider mediation, which is cheaper, faster, and often more satisfactory than court.

## Rights when interacting with police or authorities

Requirements vary widely and this is not advice for a specific situation. Broadly: you can usually ask whether you are free to leave, ask for the reason for a stop, and ask for a lawyer before answering questions in an investigation. Never lie to officials, and never physically resist. In many places you must give your name and identification in specific circumstances, and you have the right to remain silent beyond that. If you believe your rights were violated, write everything down immediately and seek local advice.
