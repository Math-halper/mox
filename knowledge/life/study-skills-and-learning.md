# Study skills and learning

Learning is a skill. The methods with the strongest evidence are effortful and sometimes feel slower than rereading — which is exactly why they work.

## How memory works

- Encoding: information enters working memory, which is small and brief. Attention is the gate.
- Consolidation: sleep and spaced review move knowledge into long-term memory.
- Retrieval: pulling information out strengthens memory more than putting it in again.
- Forgetting is normal and predictable; review timing matters more than total hours.

## High-value techniques

1. Retrieval practice. Close the book and write what you remember, then check. Use flashcards for discrete facts and free recall for explanations.
2. Spaced repetition. Review after roughly 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 3 weeks. Adjust so each review is slightly effortful.
3. Interleaving. Mix problem types so you must decide which method applies — closer to exams than blocked practice.
4. Elaboration. Ask why and how; connect new material to what you already know; generate your own examples.
5. Concrete examples. Abstract rules become usable when attached to specific cases.
6. Dual coding. Pair words with diagrams, timelines, or sketches.
7. Teach it. Explain to an imaginary beginner; gaps become obvious (the Feynman technique).

## Low-value habits to drop

Highlighting and rereading feel productive but produce weak learning. Copying notes verbatim is transcription, not thought. Cramming works briefly and fails at retention. Multi-tasking splits attention and slows both tasks.

## Reading technical material

Preview, then read actively: convert headings into questions, define terms, work the examples yourself before reading the solution, and summarize each section in one sentence. For math, do not read solutions as if they were prose — reproduce each step on paper.

## Problem solving

- Understand: restate the problem, list givens and the goal.
- Plan: choose a strategy — similar problem, simpler case, work backward, draw a diagram, guess and check, break into subproblems.
- Execute, checking units and magnitudes along the way.
- Review: does the answer make sense? What generalizes? What went wrong if it did not?

When stuck: write out what you know, find the precise point of confusion, look up one specific thing, take a short break, then return. Struggling productively before asking for help improves retention — but do ask before frustration becomes despair.

## Note-taking

Notes should be a tool for thinking, not an archive. Use your own words, capture the structure of the argument, leave room for questions, and date entries. The Cornell layout (notes, cues, summary) encourages later self-testing. Digital notes should be searchable and tagged by topic.

## Managing time and attention

- Break work into 25–50 minute focused blocks with real breaks.
- Start with the hardest task when energy is highest.
- Put the phone in another room; notifications cost more than they seem.
- Plan the week, not just the day; leave buffer for the unexpected.
- Use a simple task list with three priorities per day.
- Procrastination is usually emotional avoidance: shrink the first step to something trivially easy.

## Exams

Practice under realistic conditions (time limits, no notes). Build a one-page summary from memory at the end of a study cycle. In the exam, read all questions first, budget time, start with what you know, show reasoning for partial credit, and check arithmetic and units.

## Feedback and mindset

Seek specific feedback, not reassurance. Treat errors as information about what to practice next. "I can't do this yet" is more accurate than "I'm bad at this" — ability is trainable, though not infinitely and not at the same rate for everyone. Effort without strategy does not help; strategy plus effort does.

## Environment

Sleep, movement, and food are study infrastructure. A quiet, well-lit space with materials ready reduces the activation cost of starting. Study with others to explain and be questioned, but protect time for solitary retrieval.
