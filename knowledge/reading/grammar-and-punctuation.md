# Grammar and punctuation

Grammar is the system of rules that makes sentences understandable. Punctuation marks the boundaries and relationships that speech would carry through pauses and intonation.

## Parts of speech

- Noun: person, place, thing, idea (teacher, city, justice).
- Pronoun: replaces a noun (he, they, which, someone).
- Verb: action or state (run, is, seemed).
- Adjective: modifies a noun (bright, careful).
- Adverb: modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb (quickly, very, often).
- Preposition: shows relationship (in, on, between, despite).
- Conjunction: joins (and, but, because, although).
- Interjection: exclamation (hey, alas).

## Sentence structure

- Simple sentence: one independent clause. "The dog barked."
- Compound: two independent clauses joined by a comma and a coordinating conjunction, or a semicolon. "The dog barked, and the cat fled."
- Complex: an independent clause plus a dependent clause. "When the dog barked, the cat fled."
- Compound-complex: both patterns together.

Every sentence needs a subject and a predicate, and every clause needs a verb. A fragment is missing one; a run-on joins clauses with no connector; a comma splice joins them with only a comma.

## Agreement and consistency

Subjects and verbs agree in number: "The list of items is long" (the subject is list, not items). Pronouns agree with their antecedents: "Each student must bring their own" (singular they is standard modern usage). Keep tense consistent unless time has actually shifted.

## Commonly confused words

- their / there / they're: possession / place / they are.
- your / you're: possession / you are.
- its / it's: possession / it is.
- affect / effect: verb (to influence) / noun (result).
- then / than: time / comparison.
- fewer / less: countable items / mass quantities.
- lie / lay: to recline / to place something.
- who / whom: subject / object.
- complement / compliment: completes / praises.
- principal / principle: main or school leader / rule or value.
- imply / infer: the speaker implies; the listener infers.
- comprise / compose: the whole comprises the parts; the parts compose the whole.

## Punctuation

- Period: ends a declarative sentence, also used in abbreviations.
- Comma: separates items in a list, sets off introductory phrases, joins independent clauses with a conjunction, marks nonessential information, and separates direct address. Do not use a comma to join two independent clauses alone.
- Semicolon: joins closely related independent clauses and separates complex list items.
- Colon: introduces a list, explanation, or quotation after a complete sentence.
- Apostrophe: shows possession (the student's book, the students' books) and contractions (don't, it's). "Its" as possessive has no apostrophe.
- Quotation marks: enclose exact words; periods and commas usually go inside in US style. Block quotes for long passages. Italics or quotes mark titles depending on length: italics for books, films, albums; quotes for articles, chapters, short poems.
- Dash: abrupt break or emphasis. Hyphen: joins compound modifiers (well-known author).
- Parentheses: extra, nonessential information.
- Ellipsis: omitted words in a quotation.

## Capitalization

Capitalize the first word of a sentence, proper nouns and adjectives, titles of works (major words), days, months, languages, and nationalities. Do not capitalize seasons or generic job titles unless they precede a name.

## Common fixes

- Nominalization: "make a decision" → "decide".
- Passive voice when the actor matters: "Mistakes were made" hides who acted.
- Dangling modifier: "Walking home, the rain soaked me" → "Walking home, I was soaked by the rain."
- Parallel structure: "reading, writing, and to edit" → "reading, writing, and editing".
- Wordiness: "due to the fact that" → "because"; "in order to" → "to".
- Double negatives: "I don't have no time" → "I don't have any time".
