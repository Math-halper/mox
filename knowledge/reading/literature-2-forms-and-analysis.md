# Literature II: forms, genres, and close reading

Literature and rhetoric covers devices and argument. This companion is about the shapes writing takes — poetry, drama, the novel, the short story, and nonfiction — and a repeatable method for analysing any of them without wandering into vague opinion.

## A method for close reading

1. Read once for what happens, without a pen.
2. Read again with a question: what is this text doing, and how?
3. Collect evidence: quotations, line numbers, page numbers. Three good pieces of evidence beat ten impressions.
4. Name the technique and then say what it does — the effect, not the label. "The short sentences speed up the narration as the panic rises" is analysis; "the author uses short sentences" is a label.
5. Build a claim that can be argued against. If nobody could disagree, it is a summary.

Two traps: plot retelling disguised as analysis, and the intentional fallacy — treating the author's biography as the final answer to what a text means.

## Poetry

Poetry compresses: it uses line breaks, sound, and rhythm as part of the meaning.

- Form: sonnet (14 lines, Shakespearean or Petrarchan rhyme schemes), haiku (three lines, 5-7-5 syllables in Japanese, looser in English), villanelle (19 lines with two refrains), ode, elegy, ballad, free verse, prose poem.
- Metre: iambic pentameter is five unstressed-stressed pairs per line. Scanning means marking stresses to see the pattern and where it breaks — and the break is usually the point.
- Rhyme: end rhyme, internal rhyme, slant rhyme, eye rhyme. Rhyme schemes are lettered abab, aabb, and so on.
- Sound devices: alliteration (repeated consonants at word starts), assonance (repeated vowels), consonance (repeated consonants anywhere), onomatopoeia, sibilance (hissing s sounds).
- Line break and enjambment: a line that ends without punctuation runs into the next, creating momentum or hesitation; a caesura is a pause inside a line.
- Speaker is not the poet: the "I" of a poem is a constructed voice.
- Volta: the turn in argument or feeling, often at line 9 in a sonnet or in the final couplet.

When analysing, ask: how does the shape of the poem on the page relate to what it says? What does the rhythm do at the emotional peak?

## Drama

A play is written to be performed, so staging is part of the meaning.

- Structure: act and scene divisions, exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution. Classical unities of time, place, and action apply to Greek and neoclassical drama.
- Dialogue carries almost everything: what characters say, to whom, and what they avoid saying.
- Soliloquy: a character alone, speaking thoughts aloud. Aside: a remark to the audience others cannot hear.
- Dramatic irony: the audience knows what a character does not.
- Stage directions are instructions and evidence: light, sound, props, and silence.
- Tragedy (protagonist falls through a flaw or fate), comedy (ends in union or restoration), history, farce, and modern forms that mix them.

Ask of any scene: who has power here, who wants what, and what changes between the first and last line?

## The novel and the short story

The novel's scale permits multiple plotlines, developed settings, and change over time. The short story is built for a single effect: one turn, one revelation, one compressed moment.

Elements to analyse:

- Point of view: first person (I), second (you, rare and unsettling), third limited (inside one character), third omniscient (a narrator who knows all). Free indirect discourse slides the narrator into a character's voice without quotation marks.
- Narrator reliability: a narrator who is biased, naive, or lying changes what the reader can trust.
- Character: round versus flat, static versus dynamic, revealed through action, speech, thought, appearance, and what others say.
- Setting: place as pressure on characters, and as a symbol.
- Plot versus story: story is chronological events; plot is the arrangement the author chooses, including flashback and foreshadowing.
- Conflict: person against person, society, nature, self, technology, or fate.
- Theme: the idea the text argues about, expressed as a sentence, not a single word.
- Motif: a recurring image or phrase. Symbol: a concrete thing carrying abstract meaning within the text.
- Irony: verbal (saying the opposite), situational (the reverse of expectation), dramatic (audience knows more).

## Narrative technique in nonfiction

Memoir, essay, reportage, and history all use the tools of fiction — scene, pacing, selection, voice — while claiming truth. Judging them means checking: what is documented, what is reconstructed, whose perspective is centred, and what evidence the writer offers. Narrative history and analytical history answer different questions; a good historical writer tells you which they are doing.

## Comparative analysis

When comparing two texts:

- Choose a shared question: how each handles loss, how each uses silence, how each imagines the city.
- Compare like with like: a poem's line break against another poem's line break, not against a novel's chapter.
- Organise by idea, not by text: one paragraph per point with evidence from both, rather than "first text, then second text".
- Conclude with what the comparison reveals that neither text shows alone.

## Writing about literature

A workable structure for an essay:

- Thesis: one arguable sentence naming the text, the technique, and the effect.
- Body paragraphs: claim, evidence (quoted), analysis of how the language works, link to the thesis.
- Counter-move: acknowledge a plausible alternative reading and answer it.
- Conclusion: what the reader now understands that they did not before.

Quote briefly and integrate quotations into your own sentence. Cite the edition and line or page. Use the present tense for events in the text ("Hamlet hesitates", not "Hamlet hesitated").

## Genres and their conventions

- Epic: a long narrative of a hero's deeds, opening in medias res with an invocation.
- Romance in the medieval sense: quest, marvel, and idealised love.
- Gothic: decayed settings, secrets, doubling, and terror.
- Realism and naturalism: ordinary lives, social detail; naturalism adds determinism.
- Modernism: fragmentation, interiority, and experiment with form.
- Magical realism: the impossible treated as ordinary within a realistic setting.
- Science fiction and fantasy: a novella-length idea or a constructed world, explored through character.
- Detective fiction: a puzzle with a fair-play solution; noir adds compromised morality.
- Coming-of-age: a protagonist's understanding of the world changes.

Knowing conventions lets you describe how a text uses them and where it breaks them — which is usually where the interesting work is.

## A vocabulary for evaluation

Precision beats adjectives. Instead of "very effective imagery", say "the recurring image of water turns the house into something that swallows the family". Instead of "the tone is sad", name how: elegiac, wry, bitter, resigned, deflating. When you use a term such as irony or symbolism, be able to point at the words that produce it.

## Reading difficult texts

Slow down at the sentence that resists you and copy it out by hand. Look up unfamiliar allusions. Read an introduction for context, then decide whether you agree with it. Rereading is not failure; it is the method. If a text still resists, write down the three questions you cannot answer — that list is your essay.
