# Literature and rhetoric

Literature is the art of language; rhetoric is the study of how language persuades. Both are about craft and effect, not just content.

## Elements of fiction

- Plot: exposition, inciting incident, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution. Conflict may be person versus person, nature, society, or self.
- Character: protagonist, antagonist, foil, dynamic (changes) versus static, round versus flat. Indirect characterization shows through speech, action, thought, and others' reactions.
- Setting: time and place, which can act as atmosphere or symbol.
- Point of view: first person, second person, third-person limited, third-person omniscient, objective. The narrative distance shapes what the reader can know.
- Theme: the underlying idea about life or human nature, expressed through the pattern of the story rather than a moral stated outright.
- Symbol: a concrete object carrying abstract meaning. Motif: a repeated image or idea.
- Irony: verbal (saying the opposite), situational (outcome contradicts expectation), dramatic (reader knows more than the characters).

## Poetry

- Form: sonnet (14 lines, often iambic pentameter), haiku (5-7-5 syllables), free verse, villanelle, ode, elegy, ballad.
- Meter: stressed and unstressed syllables. Iambic pentameter is five iambs per line ("shall I compare thee to a summer's day").
- Rhyme: end rhyme, internal rhyme, slant rhyme, blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter).
- Sound devices: alliteration (repeated initial consonants), assonance (repeated vowels), consonance (repeated consonants), onomatopoeia, sibilance.
- Figurative language: metaphor (direct comparison), simile (using like or as), personification, hyperbole, understatement, metonymy (calling a thing by something associated: "the crown" for royalty), synecdoche (part for whole).

## Drama

Acts and scenes, dialogue, stage directions, soliloquy (a character alone speaking thoughts), aside (comment to the audience), dramatic irony, tragedy (a fall driven by a fatal flaw or circumstances), comedy. Read stage directions closely — they carry information dialogue does not.

## Nonfiction and rhetoric

Purpose, audience, and context determine every choice. Prove an argument through ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (logic). A strong writer balances all three; using only pathos often signals weak evidence.

Rhetorical devices and effects:
- Anaphora: repetition at the start of successive clauses ("We shall fight…").
- Antithesis: contrasting ideas in balanced phrases.
- Tricolon: rule of three.
- Rhetorical question: question asked for effect.
- Juxtaposition: placing unlike things side by side.
- Anecdote: short illustrative story.
- Concession: acknowledging an opposing point before rebutting.
- Hyperbole and litotes (deliberate understatement via negation).
- Parallelism: repeated grammatical structure.

## How to analyze a passage

1. Identify what is being said and who is speaking to whom.
2. Name the literary or rhetorical device precisely.
3. Quote a short phrase.
4. Explain how the device works.
5. State the effect: on meaning, tone, character, theme, or the reader's judgment.

Avoid device-spotting without purpose. "The author uses imagery" says nothing; "The repeated smell of smoke links the kitchen to the war the narrator fled" says something.

## Context and interpretation

Historicism reads a text within its era; formalism focuses on the text itself; psychoanalytic, feminist, Marxist, and postcolonial readings ask different questions of the same work. Multiple valid interpretations exist when they are supported by evidence from the text — it is not true that any reading is equally valid.

## Literary periods, roughly

Classical antiquity, medieval, Renaissance (14th–17th century), Enlightenment, Romanticism (emphasis on emotion and nature, late 18th–19th century), Realism, Modernism (experimentation, early 20th century), Postmodernism (irony, fragmentation, self-reference), and contemporary writing.

## Close reading checklist

Speakers and situation; tone; diction (word choice); syntax; imagery; figures of speech; sound; structure and line breaks; allusions; ending. Then ask: what does this passage do that a paraphrase would lose?
