# Reading comprehension

Comprehension is building meaning from text: decoding words, connecting ideas, and checking understanding against the author's purpose.

## The three-level model

1. Literal — what the text says directly. Facts, names, dates, sequence of events.
2. Inferential — what the text implies. Motives, causes, attitudes, predictions.
3. Evaluative — how good, fair, or persuasive the text is. Judging evidence, bias, and craft.

Strong readers move between levels instead of only hunting for literal answers.

## A repeatable reading routine

- Preview: title, headings, images, first and last paragraphs, bolded terms.
- Question: turn headings into questions you expect to answer.
- Read in chunks and pause to summarize each section in one sentence, in your own words.
- Annotate: mark claims, evidence, unfamiliar words, and places where you disagree.
- Recite and review: close the text and explain the structure and main argument aloud.

## Finding the main idea

The main idea is the claim the whole passage supports — not the topic, and usually broader than any single detail. Look at the title, the first and last sentences of paragraphs, repeated concepts, and what most details explain. Supporting details answer who, what, when, where, why, and how.

Common question forms: "The primary purpose of the passage is…", "Which statement best summarizes…", "The author mentions X in order to…".

## Inference

An inference is a conclusion supported by evidence plus reasoning. Valid inferences are testable against the text. Watch for:
- Cause and effect signposts: because, therefore, as a result, consequently, since.
- Contrast signposts: however, although, nevertheless, on the other hand, despite.
- Emphasis signposts: most importantly, above all, in fact, indeed.
- Time signposts: meanwhile, afterward, previously, finally.

## Facts, opinions, and claims

A fact can be verified; an opinion expresses judgment. A claim is an assertion that needs support. Distinguish evidence (data, examples, quotations, studies) from commentary (interpretation). Ask whether the evidence actually supports the claim, whether it is representative, and whether contrary evidence is ignored.

## Tone, purpose, and audience

Tone is the author's attitude: neutral, critical, nostalgic, urgent, ironic. Purpose is usually to inform, explain, persuade, entertain, or instruct. Audience shapes vocabulary, assumed background knowledge, and how much evidence is offered.

## Text structures

- Chronological: events in time order.
- Cause and effect: why something happened and what followed.
- Compare and contrast: similarities and differences.
- Problem and solution: a difficulty and proposed fixes.
- Description: features of a subject.
- Argument: claim, reasons, evidence, counterargument, rebuttal.

Recognizing structure helps you predict where information sits and where answers to questions will be found.

## Vocabulary in context

Use context clues: definitions in apposition, examples, contrasts, and word parts. Confirm with a dictionary afterward. Replace the unknown word with your guess and reread the sentence — if meaning holds, the guess is probably right.

## Reading difficult texts

- Slow down on the first paragraph and the conclusion.
- Break long sentences into subject, verb, and object.
- Paraphrase jargon the first time it appears.
- Track pronoun references ("this", "they", "it") to what exactly they point at.
- Note where you lose the thread; that is usually where an unfamiliar concept is introduced.
- Read a second time for structure rather than detail.

## Reading digital and multimodal texts

Check the source and date, separate advertising from content, follow links to primary sources, and read charts carefully: title, axis labels, units, scale, and starting point. A truncated y-axis exaggerates small differences.

## Study strategies that work

- Retrieval practice: quiz yourself instead of rereading.
- Spaced review: revisit material after a day, a week, and a month.
- Elaboration: explain why and how, connect to what you already know.
- Dual coding: combine words with diagrams.
- Interleaving: mix problem types rather than blocking one kind.
