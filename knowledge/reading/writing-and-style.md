# Writing and style

Good writing is clear, organized, and appropriate for its reader. Style is not decoration; it is the set of choices that makes meaning easy to follow.

## The writing process

1. Clarify purpose and audience. What should the reader know, feel, or do?
2. Plan. A thesis, an outline of main points, and the evidence each point needs.
3. Draft quickly without editing. Get the argument down.
4. Revise for structure and evidence, then for style, then for grammar.
5. Proofread separately; read aloud or read backwards to catch typos.
6. Get feedback and cut about 10% of the words.

## Paragraphs

A paragraph makes one point. Typical shape: topic sentence, support (evidence, example, explanation), then a closing or transitional sentence. Aim for variety in length; very long paragraphs bury their point.

## Essay structure

- Introduction: context, the question at issue, and a specific thesis.
- Body paragraphs: one claim each, supported by evidence, with reasoning that connects evidence to claim.
- Counterargument and rebuttal: acknowledge the strongest opposing view and answer it.
- Conclusion: implications, not a repetition of the introduction.

A thesis is arguable and specific. "Social media is bad" is a topic; "Short-form video rewards outrage, which makes civic disagreement harder to sustain" is a thesis.

## Argument and evidence

Use the claim–evidence–reasoning pattern. Types of evidence: statistics, examples, expert testimony, primary documents, experiments, and analogy. Tests for evidence:
- Relevance: does it actually bear on the claim?
- Representativeness: is the sample typical?
- Recency: is it current enough?
- Authority: what is the source's expertise?
- Sufficiency: is one example enough?

Avoid fallacies: straw man, ad hominem, false dilemma, slippery slope, appeal to authority without expertise, circular reasoning, hasty generalization, and post hoc (assuming sequence implies cause).

## Clarity techniques

- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs: "The committee rejected the plan" over "A rejection of the plan was made."
- Keep the main verb near the subject.
- Put old information first and new information at the end of a sentence.
- Cut hedges and filler: very, really, quite, actually, basically, in my opinion.
- Use signposts: first, however, therefore, for example, in contrast, as a result.
- Vary sentence length; short sentences land points.
- Define terms the reader may not share.

## Voice and tone

Voice is the writer's personality on the page; tone is the attitude toward the subject and reader. Match formality to the situation: a lab report, a cover letter, and a text message follow different conventions. Prefer plain language unless the genre requires technical vocabulary.

## Editing checklist

- Does every paragraph have a job?
- Is the main claim stated once, clearly?
- Is each claim supported?
- Are transitions doing real work?
- Any repeated sentence openings?
- Any sentence longer than about 30 words that could split?
- Are citations consistent with the required style (APA, MLA, Chicago)?

## Genres at a glance

- Summary: neutral, faithful condensation of source ideas.
- Analysis: breaks a text or situation into parts and explains how they work.
- Persuasive essay: takes a position and defends it.
- Narrative: tells a story with scene, character, and change.
- Report: presents findings objectively with methods and data.
- Email or memo: states the ask in the first two sentences.
- Technical instructions: numbered steps, one action per step, warnings first.

## Format and citation basics

Quote exactly and sparingly, introduce quotations, and explain them afterward. Paraphrase in your own structure, not just swapped synonyms. Cite every borrowed idea. Common styles: APA (social sciences: Author, year), MLA (humanities: Author page), Chicago (notes and bibliography).
