# Astronomy and space

Astronomy studies objects beyond Earth; space science studies how to operate there.

## Scale

Distances: the astronomical unit (AU) is the Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km. A light-year is about 9.46 trillion km. The nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, is about 4.24 light-years away. The Milky Way is roughly 100,000 light-years across.

## The solar system

Eight planets, with the Sun holding 99.8% of the system's mass.
- Terrestrial (rocky): Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars.
- Gas giants: Jupiter, Saturn. Ice giants: Uranus, Neptune.
- Dwarf planets include Pluto, Ceres, Eris.
- The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter; the Kuiper belt and Oort cloud hold icy bodies beyond Neptune.
- Moons orbit planets; Jupiter and Saturn have dozens each. Saturn's rings are ice and rock particles.

Planet facts: Jupiter is the largest; Venus is the hottest (runaway greenhouse); Mars has the tallest volcano (Olympus Mons) and largest canyon system; Mercury has the most extreme day–night temperature swing.

## The Sun and stars

The Sun is a main-sequence star powered by hydrogen fusion in its core, converting about 4 million tonnes of mass to energy per second. Its layers: core, radiative zone, convective zone, photosphere, chromosphere, corona. Sunspots follow an 11-year cycle; solar wind and flares drive auroras and can disrupt electronics.

Stars form from collapsing clouds of gas and dust. Their life paths depend on mass: low-mass stars become red giants then white dwarfs; massive stars undergo supernovae and leave neutron stars or black holes. The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram plots luminosity against temperature.

Spectral classes (hot to cool): O, B, A, F, G, K, M. Our Sun is a G-type star.

## Galaxies and the universe

Galaxies are gravitationally bound systems of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter: spiral, elliptical, and irregular. The Milky Way is a barred spiral. Galaxies group into clusters and superclusters.

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old. It is expanding; the farther a galaxy, the faster it recedes (Hubble's law). Cosmic microwave background radiation is the afterglow of the early universe. Dark matter (about 27% of content) and dark energy (about 68%) remain unexplained; ordinary matter is roughly 5%.

## Light and observation

Telescopes gather light; bigger apertures resolve finer detail and see fainter objects. Observatories use optical, radio, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray instruments. Spectroscopy reveals composition and motion through emission and absorption lines; redshift indicates recession.

Space telescopes avoid atmospheric blur and can observe wavelengths absorbed by air. Adaptive optics and interferometry also improve resolution.

## Orbits and spaceflight

Orbital velocity near Earth is about 7.9 km/s; escape velocity is about 11.2 km/s. Rockets work by expelling mass (Newton's third law). Staging sheds empty mass. Low Earth orbit is roughly 400 km up; geostationary orbit is about 35,786 km above the equator with a 24-hour period.

Kepler's laws: orbits are ellipses; a planet sweeps equal areas in equal times; the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis (T² ∝ a³).

Lagrange points are gravitational balance points; L1 and L2 are useful for solar observatories and space telescopes. Travel to Mars takes roughly 7–9 months with current technology, and launch windows recur about every 26 months.

## Space hazards and daily life in orbit

Microgravity causes bone and muscle loss and fluid shifts; astronauts exercise daily. Radiation, micrometeoroids, and thermal extremes require shielding. Space debris threatens satellites; most objects in orbit are tracked but many small fragments are not.

## Key numbers

Speed of light: 299,792 km/s. Earth's orbital speed: about 30 km/s. A day is 24 hours (23h 56m sidereal); a year is 365.25 days. The Moon is about 384,400 km away and causes tides through gravitational pull.
