# Chemistry II: reactions, solutions, and organic basics

Chemistry I covers atoms, bonding, and the periodic table. This companion document is about what substances do: how reactions behave, how solutions and acids work, and the small set of organic families that explain fuels, plastics, and living things.

## Reading and balancing equations

A chemical equation must have the same number of each kind of atom on both sides; matter is not created or destroyed. Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.

Common reaction types:

- Synthesis: A + B → AB, e.g. 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl.
- Decomposition: AB → A + B, e.g. 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ with a catalyst.
- Single replacement: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one.
- Double replacement: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃. Often recognised by a precipitate, gas, or water forming.
- Combustion: fuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O for hydrocarbons. Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide, which is why unvented heaters are dangerous.

## Reaction rates and energy

Rate rises with higher temperature (more collisions with enough energy), higher concentration or pressure, greater surface area (powder reacts faster than a block), and catalysts, which lower the activation energy without being consumed.

Exothermic reactions release energy to the surroundings and feel warm (combustion, neutralisation). Endothermic reactions absorb it and feel cold (baking soda plus vinegar, melting ice). Bond breaking costs energy; bond forming releases it; the net difference decides the sign.

## Solutions, moles, and concentration

A solute dissolves in a solvent to give a solution. Molarity is moles of solute per litre of solution, written M. One mole is 6.022 × 10²³ particles and, in grams, equals the molar mass.

Dilution rule: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. Adding water changes volume, not the number of moles.

Solubility rises with temperature for most solids but falls for gases, which is why warm soda goes flat. "Like dissolves like": polar solvents such as water dissolve ionic and polar substances, while oils, non-polar, dissolve in non-polar solvents such as hexane.

## Acids, bases, and pH

- Acids donate protons (H⁺) and taste sour; examples are hydrochloric, citric, and acetic acid.
- Bases accept protons, feel slippery, and include hydroxides and ammonia.
- pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]: below 7 is acidic, 7 neutral at 25 °C, above 7 basic. Each unit is a tenfold change, so pH 3 is a hundred times more acidic than pH 5.
- Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water, e.g. HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O.
- Buffers resist pH change by pairing a weak acid with its conjugate base; blood is buffered near pH 7.4.

Safety: always add acid to water, never water to acid, and wear eye protection.

## Organic families worth knowing

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon, which bonds to itself in chains and rings.

- Alkanes: single bonds, saturated, formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ — methane, propane, octane. Used as fuels.
- Alkenes: one double bond, CₙH₂ₙ — ethene, the feedstock for polyethylene plastic.
- Alkynes: one triple bond, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ — ethyne (acetylene) for welding.
- Alcohols: an −OH group — ethanol (drinking alcohol, solvent, fuel), methanol (toxic).
- Carboxylic acids: a −COOH group — acetic acid in vinegar, citric acid in fruit.
- Esters: formed from alcohol plus acid, responsible for fruity smells.
- Amines and amides: nitrogen-containing, the backbone of proteins and many drugs.
- Polymers: monomers joined into long chains — polyethylene, polystyrene, PVC, and natural polymers such as starch, cellulose, and proteins. Recycling codes matter because different polymers melt and reprocess differently.

Isomers share a formula but differ in arrangement: ethanol and dimethyl ether are both C₂H₆O yet behave completely differently, which is why structure, not formula alone, predicts properties.

## Everyday chemistry

Soap works because one end of the molecule is attracted to water and the other to grease, letting water carry oil away. Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) releases CO₂ when it meets acid or heat, which leavens batter. Rust is iron oxidising in the presence of water and oxygen; paint, galvanising, and alloying slow it. Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium, which react with soap and form scale in kettles.
