# Physics II: electricity, waves, and modern physics

Physics I covers motion, forces, energy, and heat. This companion covers the two other pillars of classical physics — electricity and magnetism, and waves — plus the modern physics that explains atoms, nuclei, and semiconductors.

## Electric charge and force

Charge comes in two signs and is conserved. Coulomb's law gives the force between two charges: F = k q₁q₂/r², an inverse-square law like gravity. Unlike charges attract, like charges repel. Conduction moves charge through a material; induction rearranges it without contact, which is why a charged rod attracts a neutral scrap of paper.

The electric field E at a point is the force per unit charge, and field lines run from positive to negative. Voltage (potential difference) is energy per unit charge: one volt is one joule per coulomb.

## Circuits

Ohm's law: V = IR. Resistance in series adds (R = R₁ + R₂); in parallel the reciprocals add (1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂), and current divides. Kirchhoff's rules formalise it: current into a junction equals current out, and voltages around a loop sum to zero.

Electric power: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. A device rated 1,000 W on 120 V draws 8.3 A.

Household wiring runs in parallel so each appliance gets full voltage and can switch independently. Fuses and circuit breakers interrupt current before wiring overheats; residual-current and ground-fault devices detect current leaking to earth. Short circuits bypass resistance and draw dangerous current.

## Magnetism and induction

Moving charges create magnetic fields; magnetic fields exert forces on moving charges. A current-carrying wire in a field experiences a force, which is the basis of motors. A changing magnetic field induces a voltage in a conductor (Faraday's law), which is the basis of generators and transformers. Transformers change AC voltage: step-up for transmission at high voltage to reduce losses, step-down for use.

## Waves

A wave transfers energy without transferring matter. Amplitude is size, wavelength is distance between crests, frequency is cycles per second (hertz), and period is seconds per cycle. Wave speed v = fλ. For sound, speed is about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, faster in water and steel.

- Reflection: angle of incidence equals angle of reflection.
- Refraction: bending when speed changes at a boundary, caused by a change in medium. It is why a straw looks bent in water and why lenses work.
- Diffraction: bending around obstacles and spreading through gaps, pronounced when the gap is about the wavelength.
- Interference: waves add (constructive) or cancel (destructive). Noise-cancelling headphones use destructive interference.
- Standing waves and resonance: fixed boundaries produce preferred frequencies; a system driven at its natural frequency resonates, which can shatter glass or collapse a bridge.

Sound: pitch is frequency, loudness relates to amplitude (decibels are logarithmic), timbre comes from the mix of overtones. The Doppler effect raises pitch as a source approaches and lowers it as it recedes — used by radar, medical ultrasound, and weather tracking.

Light: the visible spectrum is roughly 400–700 nanometres, with red the longest wavelength and violet the shortest. White light separates by refraction into colours. Polarisation constrains the oscillation to one plane; polarising filters cut glare. Light is fast: 299,792,458 m/s in vacuum.

Optics: convex lenses converge and can form real images (cameras, eyes); concave lenses diverge. Focal length, object distance, and image distance follow the lens equation 1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i, with magnification m = −d_i/d_o. Total internal reflection traps light inside fibre optics.

## Electricity and the modern world

Semiconductors — silicon, germanium — conduct only under conditions, and doping creates n-type (extra electrons) and p-type (missing electrons, "holes") material. A p–n junction is a diode: current flows one way. A transistor is a switch built from junctions, and millions or billions of them on a chip make processors and memory. Photovoltaic cells use the same junction to turn light into current.

## Modern physics

Relativity: the speed of light is the same for all observers, and time and length change with relative motion. E = mc² says mass is a form of energy, which is why nuclear processes release so much.

Quantum basics: energy comes in discrete packets (quanta). Light behaves as both wave and particle. Electrons occupy orbitals with quantised energy levels, and an atom emits or absorbs light of specific frequencies when an electron moves between them — the source of spectral lines.

The uncertainty principle says position and momentum cannot both be known precisely at once. Wave–particle duality applies to electrons, neutrons, and atoms.

Radioactivity: unstable nuclei decay by alpha (helium nucleus), beta (electron or positron), or gamma (high-energy photon) emission. Half-life is the time for half a sample to decay. Carbon-14 dating works because living things exchange carbon with the atmosphere, and decay begins at death.

Fission splits heavy nuclei and releases energy; reactors control the chain reaction with moderators and control rods, and produce long-lived waste. Fusion joins light nuclei, powers stars, and has not yet produced net electricity on Earth, though it has reached net energy gain in laboratory experiments.

Particle physics: the standard model describes quarks (which make protons and neutrons), leptons (electrons, neutrinos), and force carriers (photons, gluons, W and Z bosons), with the Higgs field giving particles mass. Gravity is explained by general relativity rather than the standard model, and unifying them is still open.

## Units and scales worth remembering

Metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela. Prefixes: kilo 10³, mega 10⁶, giga 10⁹, milli 10⁻³, micro 10⁻⁶, nano 10⁻⁹. Atomic scale is about 10⁻¹⁰ m; a nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m; the speed of sound in air is about 343 m/s; visible light has a wavelength of hundreds of nanometres.

## Thinking like a physicist

Estimate first with units and orders of magnitude. Check whether an answer is plausible. Track what is conserved — energy, momentum, charge — because conservation often solves a problem faster than forces. Distinguish models from reality: a frictionless plane is a model, and it is useful because it isolates one effect.
