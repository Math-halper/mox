# Popular websites and online services

This reference describes well-known websites and apps: what each one is for, who runs it, and how it fits into the web. It is a map, not an advertisement — none of these are required to use the internet, and most have open alternatives.

Two cautions. Sites change hands, rebrand, and shut down, so treat a specific ownership detail as a snapshot rather than a permanent fact. And "popular" is not the same as "best": for any job here there are usually smaller, calmer, or more private alternatives.

## Search and reference

- **Google** — the largest search engine, founded in 1998 by Larry Page and Sergey Brin. Its index answers questions with ten blue links, but also with direct answers, maps, shopping results, and videos. Google also runs Gmail, Google Maps, Google Docs, Android, Chrome, and YouTube.
- **Bing** — Microsoft's search engine. Its results power several other assistants and browsers, and it bundles rewards points and AI chat.
- **DuckDuckGo** — a search engine that does not build advertising profiles of its users. It draws on other indexes rather than crawling the whole web itself.
- **Baidu** — the dominant search engine in China, with its own encyclopedia, maps, and cloud storage.
- **Yandex** — a Russian search and technology company with search, maps, mail, and translation.
- **Wikipedia** — a free encyclopedia written by volunteers and run by the Wikimedia Foundation, a non-profit. Articles are editable by anyone and cite their sources. It exists in over 300 languages, and English alone holds millions of articles. Use the references at the bottom of an article to check facts.
- **Wiktionary**, **Wikidata**, and **Wikimedia Commons** — companion projects: a dictionary, a machine-readable fact database, and a library of freely licensed images.
- **Internet Archive** — a non-profit library that keeps copies of web pages. Its **Wayback Machine** shows what a URL looked like on a given date, which makes it the standard tool for citing a page that has since changed.
- **Wolfram Alpha** — a computational engine: type an equation, a unit conversion, or a city's population and it calculates rather than searches.
- **IMDb** — a database of films, television, and the people who make them. Owned by Amazon.
- **Google Maps** and **OpenStreetMap** — mapping services. OpenStreetMap is the open, volunteer-built map data that many apps and researchers reuse.
- **Quora** and **Stack Exchange** — question-and-answer sites — broad discussion on Quora, tight technical answers on Stack Exchange's many topic sites.

## Video, social, and messaging

- **YouTube** — video hosting, founded in 2005 and bought by Google in 2006. Anyone can publish; the site recommends, monetises through advertising, and offers live streaming. It also works as a music player, a lecture archive, and a how-to library.
- **TikTok** — short vertical videos with a fast, personalised feed, run by the Chinese company ByteDance. Its sibling app in China is Douyin.
- **Instagram** — photos and short videos, owned by Meta. Started in 2010 as a square-photo app.
- **Facebook** — the largest social network, launched in 2004 by Mark Zuckerberg; the company renamed itself Meta in 2021 to reflect its focus on virtual reality.
- **WhatsApp** — messaging over the internet, bought by Facebook in 2014. **Telegram** and **Signal** are rivals; Signal is the privacy-focused one, with open-source clients and end-to-end encryption on by default.
- **X** — the platform formerly called Twitter, launched in 2006 as short text posts ("tweets"), bought by Elon Musk in 2022 and renamed in 2023. Public conversation, news, and arguments.
- **Reddit** — forums ("subreddits") for every imaginable topic, founded in 2005. Upvotes sort the discussion rather than an editor.
- **Snapchat** — disappearing messages and stories, popular with younger users.
- **Pinterest** — visual bookmarks arranged on boards; strong for design, recipes, and crafts.
- **LinkedIn** — professional profiles, job listings, and workplace posts. Owned by Microsoft since 2016.
- **Discord** — voice, video, and text chat servers, originally for gamers and now used by clubs, classes, and open-source projects.
- **Twitch** — live streaming, mainly games, with live chat. Owned by Amazon.
- **Tumblr** and **Mastodon** — blogging-style social. Mastodon is decentralised: thousands of independent servers ("the fediverse") that talk to each other instead of one company.
- **Medium** and **Substack** — writing platforms. Medium hosts essays; Substack lets writers publish newsletters and charge subscriptions.

## Shopping and marketplaces

- **Amazon** — started in 1994 as an online bookshop and grew into the biggest general store on the web, with its own logistics, cloud computing arm (AWS), and devices.
- **eBay** — auctions and second-hand sales between individuals, from 1995.
- **Etsy** — handmade, vintage, and craft supplies from small sellers.
- **Alibaba** and **AliExpress** — Chinese wholesale and retail marketplaces; **Temu**, **Shein**, and **Wish** are the newer discount-import apps.
- **Shopify** — software that lets a small shop run its own storefront instead of selling inside a marketplace.
- **Craigslist** — plain-text classifieds, city by city, unchanged for decades.
- **Booking.com**, **Airbnb**, **Expedia** — travel booking: hotels and flights, private stays, and packages.
- **Uber**, **Lyft**, **Bolt** — ride-hailing apps that match riders with drivers.
- **Yelp** and **TripAdvisor** — reviews of restaurants, shops, and attractions, written by customers.

## News and magazines

- **BBC News** — public-service broadcaster funded by a licence fee, with one of the most-read news sites in the world.
- **The New York Times**, **The Guardian**, **The Washington Post** — major newspapers with strong online editions; some articles sit behind a paywall.
- **Reuters**, **Associated Press**, **AFP** — wire services that supply raw reporting to other outlets.
- **NPR** — American public radio, free to read online.
- **Hacker News** — a famously plain link list about programming and startups, run by the venture firm Y Combinator.
- **Google News** and **Apple News** — aggregators that collect headlines from many publishers.
- How to read news well: check who published it, when, and what they cite; read past the headline; look for the primary document or the wire report; and be suspicious of a story that only one obscure site carries.

## Developer tools

- **GitHub** — hosting for Git repositories, plus issue trackers, pull requests, actions (automated builds), and pages (free static websites). Founded in 2008, owned by Microsoft since 2018.
- **GitLab** and **Bitbucket** — similar repository hosting, GitLab with a strong built-in continuous-integration pipeline.
- **Stack Overflow** — the flagship site of Stack Exchange: programming questions with accepted answers, upvoted by other developers.
- **MDN Web Docs** — Mozilla's reference for HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and browser APIs. The most reliable free source for web platform details.
- **npm** and **PyPI** — package registries for JavaScript and Python. **crates.io** does the same for Rust, **Packagist** for PHP.
- **CodePen**, **JSFiddle**, **CodeSandbox**, **StackBlitz** — edit HTML, CSS, and JavaScript in a browser tab and share the result by link.
- **Vercel**, **Netlify**, **Cloudflare Pages**, **GitHub Pages** — free or cheap static hosting, usually connected to a Git repository so every push deploys.
- **Hugging Face** — a hub for open machine-learning models, datasets, and demo apps, including the GGUF files that browser-based model runners download.
- **Can I Use** — browser support tables for web features.
- **Regex101** — test and explain regular expressions step by step.

## Work, files, and design

- **Gmail**, **Outlook.com** — webmail. **Proton Mail** is the privacy-focused option.
- **Google Drive**, **Docs**, **Sheets**, **Slides** — documents that live in the browser and are edited by several people at once.
- **Microsoft 365** — the web versions of Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Teams.
- **Dropbox**, **OneDrive**, **iCloud Drive** — file sync across devices.
- **Notion**, **Obsidian**, **Evernote** — notes and wikis; Obsidian stores plain Markdown files locally.
- **Slack**, **Microsoft Teams**, **Google Chat** — workplace chat organised into channels.
- **Zoom**, **Google Meet**, **Microsoft Teams** — video meetings.
- **Trello**, **Asana**, **Jira**, **Linear** — task boards and issue tracking for teams.
- **Airtable** — spreadsheets that behave like small databases.
- **Figma** — collaborative interface design in the browser; **Canva** — templates for posters, slides, and social images; **Adobe Creative Cloud** — professional image, video, and layout tools as subscriptions.
- **Google Analytics** — traffic statistics for websites; **Plausible** and **Matomo** are lighter, privacy-friendly alternatives.

## AI assistants and tools

- **ChatGPT** (OpenAI) — a general chat assistant built on the GPT family of models; text, images, code, and voice.
- **Claude** (Anthropic) — a chat assistant focused on long documents, writing, and code.
- **Gemini** (Google) — Google's assistant, woven into Search, Gmail, and Android.
- **Microsoft Copilot** — OpenAI models inside Windows, Office, and Edge.
- **Perplexity** — an answer engine that searches the web and cites its sources inline.
- **Midjourney**, **DALL·E**, **Stable Diffusion** — image generators. Stable Diffusion is the open one, runnable on your own hardware.
- **Ollama**, **llama.cpp**, **LM Studio** — run open language models locally, no account and no server. Mox follows the same idea inside the browser through WebAssembly.
- Practical caution: assistants sound equally confident when they are wrong. Ask for sources, check numbers, and treat generated citations as leads rather than evidence.

## Learning

- **Khan Academy** — free lessons and practice exercises from arithmetic to economics, run as a non-profit.
- **Coursera**, **edX**, **Udacity** — university courses online, some free to audit, some with paid certificates.
- **Duolingo** — short daily language exercises with streaks and points.
- **MIT OpenCourseWare** — lecture notes, problem sets, and videos from real MIT courses, free.
- **Project Gutenberg** — tens of thousands of public-domain books as plain text and EPUB.
- **JSTOR**, **Google Scholar**, **arXiv**, **PubMed** — academic search: archived journals, scholarly literature across fields, preprint physics and computer science, and biomedical papers.
- **YouTube** and **Wikipedia** — informal but genuinely useful: a lecture, a worked example, and an overview article together cover a lot of ground.

## Music, games, and entertainment

- **Spotify** — streaming music and podcasts, founded in Sweden in 2008. **Apple Music**, **YouTube Music**, **Tidal**, **Deezer** are the main alternatives.
- **SoundCloud** and **Bandcamp** — independent music. Bandcamp lets listeners pay artists directly.
- **Steam** — the biggest store and launcher for PC games, with sales, reviews, and mod workshops.
- **Epic Games Store**, **GOG**, **itch.io** — rival stores; itch.io specialises in small experimental games.
- **Roblox** and **Minecraft** — platforms where players build and share worlds.
- **Netflix**, **Disney+**, **Max**, **Prime Video**, **Hulu** — subscription streaming video. **YouTube** and **Twitch** carry the free, ad-supported side.
- **Rotten Tomatoes** and **Metacritic** — review aggregators that average critics' scores.

## What these sites have in common

Almost every site above is built from the same few parts:

1. **HTML** for structure, **CSS** for appearance, **JavaScript** for behaviour — served over HTTPS.
2. **Accounts and sessions** kept with cookies or tokens, which is why logging in on one tab logs you in on the next.
3. **A search box and a feed**: an index on the server, a ranked list on the page, and infinite scroll that fetches more items with background requests.
4. **A database and an API**: the page you see is usually rendered from JSON that the same company's mobile app also uses.
5. **A content delivery network** that caches images and scripts close to you, which is why a site loads quickly across an ocean.
6. **Recommendations** driven by what you clicked, watched, or bought — and by what people similar to you did.
7. **Progressive web app features**: install to a home screen, offline caching with a service worker, push notifications.

## Reading a site critically

- Look at the domain, not just the page: `example.com.co` is not `example.com`.
- A padlock means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.
- Prefer primary sources: the paper, the law, the documentation, the dataset, the original post.
- Watch for engagement bait — content engineered to make you angry or afraid travels further than accurate content.
- Free services are usually paid for by advertising or by data. Knowing which changes how you read them.
